# We Already Solved This
*Draft · not published*

**Too much code. Not enough shared knowledge.**

There was a particular kind of magic in typing four words into a search engine and finding a Stack Overflow thread where someone had already spent an afternoon solving your problem.

In [Syntax #1050](https://syntax.fm/show/1050/everyone-is-hacking-their-tv/transcript), CJ raises a question about what we lose when agents do the work of rediscovery for us. The code may be correct. The answer may arrive. But if the result stays in an isolated session, what happened to the shared shortcut?

This is not a complaint about agents. It is a question about infrastructure.

## Problem one: indexing
GitHub contains an extraordinary archive of solutions, but its primary organizing units are repositories, files, commits and discussions. What if we indexed the *problems solved*—along with their constraints, implementations and evidence—before anyone searched?

**Interactive experiment:** Four Words. Enter a short problem; inspect a ranked, source-linked answer. Compare it with plain keyword search. No agent wandering through a codebase at query time.

## Problem two: community
Stack Overflow was not just answers. Votes, accepted solutions, comments, edits, reputation, and moderation made collective judgement visible. Google made those conversations discoverable. Neither popularity nor a signature guarantees truth, but those signals are useful when kept inspectable.

What if a community did not have to live on one site? An author could publish on their own blog, other people could respond from their own identities, and different applications could assemble the same records as a thread, forum, or problem map.

**Interactive experiment:** One conversation, three views. The original contributions remain attributed, including disagreement. An LLM may suggest connections; it cannot silently manufacture consensus.

## This article is part of the experiment
The problem you're reading about is itself a structured, indexable problem. Its sources, proposed approaches, open questions, and future evidence should be discoverable without requiring you to visit this particular website.

We do not yet know whether this approach will work better. That is why we want to measure it in public.

**Open questions:** Can four words find the right answer? Can a discussion move between independent interfaces without losing context? Can community signals help without becoming a popularity contest?
